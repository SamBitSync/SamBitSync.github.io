What might it mean for an established position to be stable, or for a disturbance to move a system into another state? The simulation below makes those possibilities visible with a particle moving between two attractive basins.

It is a mechanical analogy. It does not calculate a Bayesian posterior, represent the content of a belief, or test how a person responds to evidence. The connection to belief revision is a question to examine through the analogy.

## What the controls change

Perturbing the system gives the particle a push. Deepening the basins strengthens their attraction. Flattening the landscape weakens it. Depending on its current position and motion, the particle may return to the same basin or move toward another.

These controls separate two ways the simulation can change: disturbing a state within a landscape, and changing the landscape itself. That distinction is the useful part of the metaphor.

## What the analogy suggests
An established interpretation can provide a way of absorbing an anomaly. Another encounter may prompt a change in the assumptions through which the anomaly was interpreted. The landscape gives me a way to ask about the difference between those changes.

The metaphor also directs attention to the route taken. A system's present state may matter to what a disturbance produces. To carry that idea into an account of belief, I would need to specify what the states and disturbances correspond to, rather than assuming that the labels do the explanatory work.
## Where it stops

The particle has no reasons, commitments, language, or social relations. A physical push has no evidential content. Calling a basin a prior does not supply a probability model, and calling a particle a posterior does not supply an update rule.

For this to become a model of belief revision, those correspondences would need to be made explicit and assessed. Until then, I use the simulation to formulate questions about stability and change. The [Ship practice](/process/ship/) records actual reconsiderations and their consequences.
