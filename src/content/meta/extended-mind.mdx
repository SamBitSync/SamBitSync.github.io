# Extended Mind

## The Idea

Clark and Chalmers' parity principle: if a process would count as cognitive were it happening in the head, it counts as cognitive when it happens partly outside it. The test case is Otto and Inga. Inga recalls MoMA's address from biological memory; Otto, who has Alzheimer's, looks it up in the notebook he always carries. The claim is that Otto knew the address before opening the notebook. It is reliably present, automatically trusted, and easily accessed, which is what his belief-storage does; the notebook is part of his cognitive system, not an aid to it.

Those criteria (constant availability, automatic endorsement, easy access) are what block the obvious objection that everything becomes part of your mind. Google does not qualify; the notebook you never leave home without might. The remaining fight is over coupling versus constitution: critics say external tools merely cause cognition without being part of it, and Clark's reply is that the brain-body boundary is causal coupling too, so the skull is not a principled border.

## Why I Keep It

It changes my unit of analysis to brain-in-context. In multilingual work the question becomes concrete: is a bilingual's dictionary use, or code-switching to L2 for numbers and technical terms, extended cognition or mere tool use? And this site is a live instance for me. With aphantasia and poor autobiographical memory, my notes do the work biological memory does for others, which makes Otto less a thought experiment than a description.

## Tensions

- Coupling versus constitution has no clean resolution; the criteria feel more like a gradient than a boundary.
- If minds extend into devices, questions about identity and privacy stop being metaphors.
